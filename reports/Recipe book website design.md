# Borrow the cookbook page, then add cook mode

Build Eric's site as a small bilingual printed cookbook first and a kitchen tool second. Each dish gets one "page". At the top is a title block with the English name, the hanzi and the pinyin with tone marks, plus Jyutping where the family uses a Cantonese name. Below it come a short headnote saying who taught the dish, a compact line with servings, time and status, ingredients grouped into numbered sets that the steps call by number (the Wei-Chuan convention), and numbered steps. The page should be plain semantic HTML that works with JavaScript off and prints on one sheet. Cook mode then sits on top as a stack of small, well-supported web features rather than one big app. In order, those are: a "keep screen on" toggle using the Screen Wake Lock API (Baseline since March 2025, about 96% support), ingredient check-off that survives a reload, tap-to-mark the current step, a full-screen view that shows one step at a time, and timers you tap to start, written into the recipe by hand. That covers "guide me through it" without native-app extras like voice control. For the look, the award evidence favours one consistent visual style and a two-colour palette over red-and-gold "Chinese" decoration. Two of the four James Beard Visuals winners from 2023 to 2026 were Chinese-diaspora books, each built around a single strong visual voice. The most praised Asian-American food brands earned that praise by refusing chop-suey lettering and stereotyped red. The richest culturally specific material is everyday Chinese and Hong Kong information design: dim sum order forms, manuscript grid paper and cha chaan teng wall menus. These map directly onto recipe UI as tick-off stamps, character grids and a daily board. Open fonts already solve the typography: a Song (Ming) face for Chinese text, a Kai (楷, brush-script) face for family notes, and a Latin serif checked to carry every pinyin tone mark. Openly licensed photos exist for all eight dishes but come with gaps and credit obligations, so treat them as stand-ins until Eric has his own reference photos or drawings. With about eight recipes, the home page is the index, and the site needs no search, ratings or "jump to recipe" button.

## One recipe, one page, numbered ingredient sets

Serious Chinese cookbooks use the title block to carry a dish's identity across languages. Fuchsia Dunlop's *The Food of Sichuan* titles each recipe in English, Chinese characters and pinyin, for example "Stir-fried Celery with Ground Pork" over 烂肉西芹 (lanrou xiqin), and her headnotes explain where a version came from and why she changed it ([WBUR](https://www.wbur.org/onpoint/2019/10/24/food-of-sichuan-fuchsia-dunlop)). Clarissa Wei's *Made in Taiwan* goes further, with **four layers: English, characters, pinyin and Tâi-lô**, the Taiwanese romanization. Jen Lin-Liu argues that translating dish names is a core design problem. She praises *A Very Chinese Cookbook* for rendering 口水鸡 (literally "saliva chicken") as "Mouthwatering Chicken" ([China Books Review](https://chinabooksreview.com/2025/08/21/chinese-cookbooks/)). For Eric this means three decisions per dish: the English display name, the hanzi as the family writes them, and a romanization. Use pinyin by default and add Jyutping for dishes the family calls by their Cantonese name, such as the pineapple bun. The headnote should carry provenance in the way *The Woks of Life* credits each recipe to a family member: Judy's heritage dishes, Bill's restaurant-style cooking, the daughters' weeknight food ([Publishers Weekly](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780593233894)).

The most useful heritage model is the Wei-Chuan cookbook series that many diaspora households owned. *Chinese Cuisine* (Huang Su-Huei, first published in Chinese around 1972) gives **221 recipes, one per page, each with a finished-dish photo and both English and Chinese text** ([The Vintage Kitchen](https://shopinthevintagekitchen.com/products/chinese-cuisine-wei-chuan-cooking-book-by-huang-su-huei-1978-edition-bi-lingual-chinese-cookbook)). Readers value that photo as a working reference, because it shows how fine to chop and how thick the sauce should be ([Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/en/book/show/657420)). In *Chinese Snacks*, a reader notes, **the ingredients come in grouped, numbered sets that are easy to follow** ([ThriftBooks](https://www.thriftbooks.com/w/chinese-snacks-wei-quan-shi-pu_wei-chuan-publishing_su-huei-huang/283375/)). This matters more than it looks. Chinese home cooking is prepared in sets: marinade, sauce, aromatics and the main ingredient each go into the wok at a different moment. When step 4 says "add ②", the cook reaches for one bowl instead of re-reading five lines. On the web, each set becomes a labelled group with a circled numeral (①②③, which every CJK font carries). The numbered set also fixes the biggest flaw in web recipes, covered in the next section: losing track of amounts while working through the method.

Page order should put the recipe first. Long blog preambles exist mainly for search ranking and ad space ([Bowl of Delicious](https://www.bowlofdelicious.com/why-are-food-blog-posts-so-long/)). Designers point to NYT Cooking as the counter-model: the recipe leads, with a photo, a short headnote and then the recipe, and no sidebar ([Cre8d Design](https://www.cre8d-design.com/2025/04/why-food-bloggers-are-pointing-to-nyt-cooking/)). Keep the headnote to two or three sentences above the ingredients. Put longer family stories after the method or in a collapsible section. Write tips and substitutions as a separate notes block, the way King Arthur separates "Tips from our Bakers" from its steps ([King Arthur](https://www.kingarthurbaking.com/recipes/classic-sandwich-bread-recipe)).

Metadata and the index should be built for retrieval, not recency. Phaidon's *China: The Cookbook* tags each recipe with its region but has no regional index. One reader built their own and found **about 30% of recipes come from Guangdong and Hong Kong** ([Hungry Onion](https://www.hungryonion.org/t/regional-breakdown-of-phaidons-china-the-cookbook/11147)). The web lesson is to make region, technique and main ingredient into links, not inert labels. *Ottolenghi SIMPLE*'s letter tags ("Short on time", "Make ahead" and so on) show what a compact tag system can do, though reviewers say it takes time to learn ([The Happy Foodie](https://thehappyfoodie.co.uk/books/simple-ottolenghi); [Critics at Large](https://www.criticsatlarge.ca/2019/01/elaborate-simplicity-yotam-ottolenghis.html?m=1)). For an archive this small, Feast, the leading food-blog theme vendor, says to **skip a dedicated recipe index under 100 posts** and to drop JavaScript dropdown filters for accessibility ([Feast](https://docs.feastdesignco.com/modern-recipe-index/)). The home page should therefore be a grid of eight cards, each answering "what is it, how long, what kind" without a click. Two or three described collections, such as "dough day" or "weeknight", fit NYT Cooking's recent redesign of collections to carry descriptions ([App Store: NYT Cooking](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/nyt-cooking-quick-tasty-meals/id911422904)).

Two structural layers sit outside individual recipes. The first is a techniques layer. Made With Lau presents itself as a living archive of the founder's father's recipes and teaches techniques and principles in lessons separate from its recipes ([Made With Lau](https://www.madewithlau.com/)). Eric's dumpling wrappers belong there as a lesson that the dumpling recipe links to, rather than steps repeated inside each recipe that uses them. The second is a pantry and equipment primer, following *The Woks of Life*'s front matter on pantry, tools and techniques ([Publishers Weekly](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780593233894)) and Wei-Chuan's photographs of unfamiliar grocery items ([Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/en/book/show/657420)).

Two tensions remain. Lin-Liu notes that precise measurements can make a Chinese recipe feel *less* authentic, because classical Chinese recipe writing varied in how much it quantified ([China Books Review](https://chinabooksreview.com/2025/08/21/chinese-cookbooks/)). The site should therefore allow sensory cues ("until the wrapper edges look translucent") alongside numbers, and treat "to taste" as a real value rather than a missing one. Print cookbooks also offer no convention for marking unfinished recipes. The closest established system is the "digital garden", which labels each note's certainty and lets notes grow through stages ([Obsidian Hub summary of Maggie Appleton](https://publish.obsidian.md/hub/05+-+Concepts/A+Brief+History+and+Ethos+of+the+Digital+Garden)). A visible status in the metadata line, running from *from memory* to *cooked once* to *tested n times* to *family-approved*, makes a public showcase honest about recipes still being worked out. It also echoes NYT Cooking's "Cooked" marker ([NYT Help Center](https://help.nytimes.com/hc/en-us/articles/19686556670868-Cooking-Recipe-Features)).

## Cook mode is a ladder of five small features

Recipe sites and apps have settled on cook mode in two tiers. The first is a single "keep screen awake" toggle: King Arthur's "Bake Mode", Paprika's "Keep Screen On", WP Recipe Maker's cook-mode toggle. The second is a focus view that adds check-off, step-by-step navigation and timers. The Kitchn's October 2025 "Cook Mode+" added an ingredient checklist and collapsible steps, presenting its old plain toggle as something it had outgrown ([The Kitchn via AOL](https://www.aol.com/articles/introducing-cook-mode-191500104.html)). Mob ties the two tiers together with one "GO" button, which starts step-by-step mode and keeps the screen awake ([Mob](https://www.mob.co.uk/recipes/mixed-berry-coulis)).

The web now supports the first tier natively. MDN marks the **Screen Wake Lock API as Baseline 2025**. It requires HTTPS, and the browser releases the lock whenever the page is hidden, so the page must request it again on `visibilitychange`. MDN also advises showing whether the lock is active and offering a way to turn it off ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Screen_Wake_Lock_API)). caniuse puts support at **about 96% globally**, including Safari 16.4+ on iOS ([caniuse](https://caniuse.com/wake-lock)). That is high enough to skip the hidden-video workarounds, which Chrome's documentation warns are fragile and drain the battery ([Chrome for Developers](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/capabilities/web-apis/wake-lock)). One implementation detail matters. Battery saver can revoke a lock the browser already granted, so the button should track the user's *intent* separately from the lock object and listen for its `release` event. Otherwise the "on" state lies.

The second tier comes from native apps, and each piece translates to a static site. Paprika lets cooks tap an ingredient to cross it off and tap a direction to highlight their place, and pinned recipes remember both ([Paprika](https://www.paprikaapp.com/help/ios/)). The Cooklang app's cooking mode fills the screen and shows one step at a time. It opens with a mise-en-place list for the current section, shows each step's ingredients at the bottom of the screen, and starts timers from the step ([Cooklang app help](https://cook.md/help/android/using-the-app)). Pestle sets each step in large type with amounts highlighted, and tapping a highlighted amount offers a timer ([MacStories](https://www.macstories.net/reviews/pestle-1-2-the-macstories-review/)). Behind all three is a problem Modus Create named in 2017: the friction is in the method section, **where the cook loses the link back to the amounts** ([Modus Create](https://moduscreate.com/blog/the-user-experience-on-recipe-sites-is-broken)). Wei-Chuan's numbered sets are the print answer. The web answer is to render each quantity inside the step text from the same data as the ingredient list, so scaling updates both places at once.

Timers deserve their own rule. Paprika auto-detects times in the text and highlights them ([Paprika](https://www.paprikaapp.com/help/ios/)). Cooklang has the author mark them explicitly, as in `~{25%minutes}` ([Cooklang spec](https://cooklang.org/docs/spec/)). For eight hand-written recipes, explicit marking avoids false positives such as "2–3 minutes per side". It also suits this particular set of dishes: half of them involve dough that rests, proofs or steams, so timers earn their keep more here than in a stir-fry collection. Scaling should follow Cooklang's conventions. Most ingredients scale linearly, some quantities are locked with `=`, and timers never scale ([Cooklang conventions](https://github.com/cooklang/spec/blob/main/conventions.md)). The cheapest way to handle units is King Arthur's: print both inline, as in "3 cups (360g)", instead of building a conversion engine ([King Arthur](https://www.kingarthurbaking.com/recipes/classic-sandwich-bread-recipe)).

Kitchen conditions set the physical spec. WCAG 2.2's enhanced target size of **44×44 CSS px** explicitly covers hand tremor, limited dexterity and use in unstable conditions. The AA minimum is 24×24 ([W3C 2.5.5](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/target-size-enhanced.html); [W3C 2.5.8](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/target-size-minimum.html)). Floury or greasy fingers argue for the larger figure, with the whole ingredient row as the tap target. Paprika's adjustable recipe font size shows that kitchen reading distance matters ([Paprika](https://www.paprikaapp.com/help/ios/)). Body text of at least 18px on phones, larger type in the focus view, dark-mode support for dim kitchens and near-AAA contrast are design judgements, not tested kitchen research. No source in the notes measured glare or counter-top reading distance.

The table orders the work by value against effort for about eight recipes. The ranking is judgement from the research, not measured data. One deliberate change: the notes' value-for-effort ranking put the one-step focus view eighth, but step-by-step guidance is the site's stated purpose, so the focus view should follow straight after the basics it builds on.

| Order | Feature | Precedent | Effort / value |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Recipe-first semantic page: meta as `<dl>`, grouped `<ul>` sets, one continuously numbered `<ol>` | NYT Cooking, King Arthur | Low / highest |
| 2 | Ingredient check-off saved in `localStorage` (keyed by recipe and content version, with a reset) | Paprika, The Kitchn | Low / high |
| 3 | "Keep screen on" toggle with honest on/off state | King Arthur Bake Mode, Mob GO | Low / high |
| 4 | Tap to mark the current step (`aria-current="step"`) | Paprika | Low / high |
| 5 | One-step focus view: big type, Prev/Next in thumb reach, "Step 3 of 9", that step's ingredient sets below, wake lock on automatically | Cooklang app, Pestle, Mob | Medium / high for this site |
| 6 | Tap-to-start timers from explicit markup, in a floating tray, with sound, vibration and a text alert | Paprika, Crouton, Cooklang | Medium / medium |
| 7 | Print stylesheet: chrome hidden, two-column ingredients, scale noted | NYT Cooking's printing from Cook Mode | Low / high |
| 8 | Scaling (½×–3×) with sane fractions and locked quantities | Paprika, NYT, Cooklang `=` | Medium / medium (dumpling batches) |
| 9 | Recipe JSON-LD generated from the same data | Google Search Central | Low / medium |

Print stays first-class. NYT Cooking recently added printing from its Cook Mode in an app release ([App Store: NYT Cooking](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/nyt-cooking-quick-tasty-meals/id911422904)), and `@page` margins have been Baseline since December 2024 ([MDN @page](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/Reference/At-rules/@page)). For structured data, Google's Recipe documentation (updated September 2026) **requires only `name` and `image`**. `HowToSection` is for genuinely grouped steps, and `recipeYield` should be a bare number of servings ([Google Search Central](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/recipe)). Guided Recipes markup for Google Assistant was withdrawn in 2024 ([Sitebulb](https://sitebulb.com/structured-data-history/?tag=Recipe)), so JSON-LD buys rich results and imports into recipe apps, not voice guidance. All of this depends on one decision that must come before recipe one. Ingredients must be structured data (quantity, unit, item, prep, set number, locked or not), whether written in Cooklang with a small custom Astro loader or in Zod-validated frontmatter with inline ingredient references. Cooklang puts amounts inline in steps by design. Frontmatter gives per-ingredient slots for grams and notes without a custom loader. One Astro 7 caveat applies to the frontmatter route: Markdown now renders through Astro's own Sätteri pipeline, so remark plugins must be ported or explicitly opted back in ([Astro v7 upgrade guide](https://docs.astro.build/en/guides/upgrade-to/v7/)).

## Song carries the text, Kai carries the family's voice

The W3C's Chinese layout requirements (clreq) assign typefaces by role. **Song/Ming is the default for body text**. Kai serves textbooks, headlines, **quotations and dialogue**. Hei (sans) is for headlines, captions and emphasis ([W3C clreq](https://www.w3.org/TR/clreq/)). That maps neatly onto a family recipe book. Hanzi in titles and steps take a Song face. Headnotes and margin notes such as "Mom says less sugar" take Kai, so the provisional, spoken layer reads like handwriting in the margin rather than instructions.

The font choice has a trap that is easy to miss. The research tested each Google Fonts family's real character coverage against the dish names. **Noto Serif TC and Noto Serif HK are missing 鸡, 饼, 萝, 锅, 贴, 饺 and 盐**, characters that appear in Eric's own Simplified dish names (盐鸡, 葱油饼, 菠萝包, 饺子). Noto Serif SC, LXGW WenKai TC, Chiron Sung HK and Chiron Hei HK passed the Simplified, Traditional, Cantonese-particle (嘅啲咗) and pinyin test strings ([Google Fonts metadata](https://fonts.google.com/metadata/fonts)). The Latin side has its own trap. Browsers fall back one glyph at a time, so a Latin face that lacks ǎ or ǚ gives a pinyin line set in two typefaces. Several fashionable faces fail this test, including Spectral, Young Serif, Gloock, Castoro, Bricolage Grotesque, Figtree and Manrope. Literata, Alegreya, Piazzolla, Brygada 1918 and Source Serif 4 pass, capitals included ([Google Fonts metadata](https://fonts.google.com/metadata/fonts)). The Simplified-Chinese calligraphic display faces (Ma Shan Zheng, ZCOOL XiaoWei and others) ship no latin-ext subset, so they cannot carry pinyin ([Google Fonts CSS API](https://fonts.googleapis.com/css2?family=Noto+Serif+SC:wght@400&display=swap)).

| Role | Recommended font | Licence and delivery | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| English text and pinyin | Literata (alternates: Alegreya for a pen-made feel, Brygada 1918 for display) | OFL, Google Fonts | Full pinyin coverage including ǖǘǚǜ and capitals; book-serif texture matches a Song face |
| Hanzi titles and steps (Simplified) | Noto Serif SC | OFL, Google Fonts, 101 slices | Passed every test string |
| Hanzi in Traditional or Hong Kong forms | Chiron Sung HK | OFL, Google Fonts | Hong Kong standard forms; still covers Simplified |
| Headnotes and family notes (Kai voice) | LXGW WenKai TC, or `lxgw-wenkai-gb-web` for mainland-standard (GB) forms | OFL; Google Fonts, or self-hosted pre-sliced build | clreq's Kai-for-quotations role; Simplified LXGW WenKai is not on Google Fonts |

The licences and packages are verified. LXGW WenKai is OFL-1.1, at v1.522 since March 2026 ([LXGW WenKai releases](https://github.com/lxgw/LxgwWenKai/releases/latest)). A current pre-sliced web build exists for self-hosting ([lxgw-wenkai-gb-web](https://github.com/CMBill/lxgw-wenkai-gb-web)).

Script is data, not a site setting. Store each dish's hanzi as the family member writes them and tag the language (`zh-Hans`, `zh-Hant`, `zh-HK`), so the right glyph forms and punctuation follow. clreq notes that **Taiwan and Hong Kong centre punctuation in the character box** and use 「」 quotes, while mainland text places punctuation at the corner and uses curly quotes ([W3C clreq](https://www.w3.org/TR/clreq/)). For spacing between Chinese and Latin text, clreq specifies a gap of no more than a quarter of a character width ([W3C clreq](https://www.w3.org/TR/clreq/)). CSS `text-autospace` now inserts it automatically and has been **Baseline since November 2025** ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/text-autospace)). The alternative is the widely used Chinese Copywriting Guidelines, which have authors type the space by hand ([chinese-copywriting-guidelines](https://github.com/sparanoid/chinese-copywriting-guidelines)). Pick one method; using both doubles the gap. Chinese paragraphs need their own rhythm: books typically run **17–40 characters per line** ([W3C clreq](https://www.w3.org/TR/clreq/)), and a common Chinese UI spec uses line-height 1.5 as regular and 1.7 as loose ([Element Plus](https://cn.element-plus.org/zh-CN/component/typography)).

Pinyin above characters is tempting but should stay at title scale. clreq places romanized annotations centred over the base text at about half its size, in a thin, sturdy sans ([W3C clreq](https://www.w3.org/TR/clreq/)). HTML `<ruby>` supports a fallback for simple ruby, where a word carries one annotation, but not for complex ruby, where each character is annotated separately ([WCAG H62](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Techniques/html/H62)). Old WHATWG discussion also records screen readers voicing both the base text and the reading ([WHATWG list](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/public-whatwg-archive/2009Oct/0622.html)). No current screen-reader test exists in the notes. Use word-level ruby on recipe titles and a separate pinyin line everywhere else.

Loading is manageable if no one ships whole font files. A single LXGW WenKai Regular TTF is **about 25 MB** ([LXGW WenKai releases](https://github.com/lxgw/LxgwWenKai/releases/latest)). Google Fonts instead serves CJK families as roughly 100–120 slices per weight, each covering a range of characters. The mean slice is about 30 KB for Noto Serif SC and about 41 KB for LXGW WenKai TC, and the browser downloads only the slices a page uses ([Google Fonts CSS API](https://fonts.googleapis.com/css2?family=Noto+Serif+SC:wght@400&display=swap)). Google's `text=` parameter cuts fixed headings by up to 90% ([Google Fonts docs](https://developers.google.com/fonts/docs/getting_started)). Because a static eight-recipe site knows every character it uses at build time, self-hosting with cn-font-split or pyftsubset is also realistic ([cn-font-split](https://www.jsdelivr.com/package/npm/cn-font-split)). The research estimated a CJK font budget of 200–500 KB per weight per page but did not measure it on a real page. Limit CJK fonts to one or two weights.

## One visual style, two colours, and no red-and-gold

The design-award record favours one authorial visual voice. *Chinese-ish* won the 2023 James Beard Visuals award ([James Beard Foundation](https://archive.jamesbeard.org/blog/the-2023-james-beard-media-award-winners)). Its illustrations and calligraphy are **treated as equal partners to the recipes** ([Colournary](https://colournary.com/stories/emerging-from-isolasian-with-rosheen-kaul)), and it began as a lockdown zine. *The Book of Sichuan Chili Crisp* won the same award in 2024 on Yudi Echevarria's photography ([Penguin Random House](https://global.penguinrandomhouse.com/announcements/our-2024-james-beard-foundation-media-award-winners/)). For *Alpine Cooking*'s AIGA-winning cover, the designer says **the display typeface set the direction** for the whole package ([Penguin Random House](https://global.penguinrandomhouse.com/announcements/50-books-50-covers-our-award-winning-designs/)). On the web that suggests a distinctive English, hanzi and pinyin title treatment can carry the site's identity with no photos at all.

Leaving out photography is a respected choice, not a compromise. Samin Nosrat chose no photos for *Salt Fat Acid Heat*, arguing that food photography stages an artificial, perfected world ([Mother Jones](https://fullsite.motherjones.com/environment/2017/04/cookbook-samin-nosrat-wendy-macnaughton-illustrated)). Carolyn Phillips's *All Under Heaven*, a survey of China's 35 regional cuisines, uses the author's own line drawings of technique and no photographs ([Eat Your Books](https://wp.eatyourbooks.com/?p=17490); [McSweeney's](https://mcsweeneys.net/books/allunderheaven)). Lucky Peach turned to illustration and flow-chart recipes because it could not afford studio photography ([Eater](https://www.eater.com/2017/4/10/15240054/lucky-peach-design-illustration)). Wei-Chuan is the counter-case, and it shows what a photo is *for*: checking cut size and sauce thickness ([Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/en/book/show/657420)). Plain, consistent reference photos of Eric's own pleats, dough thickness and sauce gloss do more work in cook mode than styled hero shots. Hand drawings of folds and wok motions can fill the gaps.

Award-platform food sites point the same way. On Awwwards, the colour palettes extracted for recent Food & Drink Sites of the Day are two colours each. Moooor is cream #FCF7EB with soft black #363636 ([Awwwards](https://www.awwwards.com/sites/moooor)). Kerrygold's "The Magical Pantry" is olive and ochre, and it recast recipes as **an interactive storybook** with gesture micro-interactions that copy real cooking motions ([Awwwards](https://www.awwwards.com/sites/kerrygold-the-magical-pantry-1); [makemepulse](https://www.makemepulse.com/case-study/kerrygold-the-magical-pantry)). NYT Cooking's 2025 card redesign removed containers and grey backgrounds under the principle that **text should support the image, not compete with it** ([NYT Open](https://medium.com/@timesopen/using-provocations-to-shake-the-status-quo-c03d5215aa25)). Hetty McKinnon's 2026 site uses hand-made illustrations of vegetables across the seasons for browsing, plus a Cook Mode ([Wonderly](https://withwonderly.com/hetty-mckinnon/)). The pattern across all of them: one paper ground, one ink, one hand.

Asian-American food brands show how to be culturally specific without costume. Omsom's founding rule ruled out anything that could read as literal or stereotyped Asian imagery. Its palette chose deep orange and yellow, **"intentionally avoiding stereotypical red"** ([Outline](https://www.weareoutline.com/case-studies/omsom)). Its in-house designer describes it as the first brand to reject chop-suey lettering and red-and-gold as the markers of an Asian brand ([Michelle Kao](https://michelleykao.com/01-Omsom)). Fly By Jing went the other way, toward loud, text-heavy, deliberately unpolished labels closer to real grocery packaging ([Fonts In Use](https://fontsinuse.com/uses/37812/fly-by-jing-packaging-and-website)). Many in the East Asian diaspora consider wonton-style "chopstick" lettering offensive ([Wikipedia: Wonton font](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wonton_font)). The most transferable rule comes from Singapore's Tong Heng bakery. Its rebrand **kept the calligraphy and logo created by the owner's aunt** and modernized everything around them ([President's Design Award](https://pda.designsingapore.org/stories/2018/redesigning-traditions-for-tomorrow/)). For Eric, a scan of a family member's handwritten dish name is worth more than any borrowed emblem.

Some material carries more risk. The red-white-blue woven bag became a kind of folk flag around the 1997 handover ([Art Basel](https://www.artbasel.com/stories/stanley-wong-hong-kong-tai-ping-carpets-walk-on)) and has derogatory names abroad ([Zolima](https://zolimacitymag.com/ghana-must-go-the-surprising-global-journey-of-hong-kongs-red-white-blue-bag/)). Use it only as a material texture, if at all. Hong Kong Beiwei signage lettering is beautiful, but the designer of the Zansyu typeface built from it criticizes nostalgic design that sells Hong Kong feeling like a tourist souvenir ([Constance Tang](https://constance-tang.com/2021/10/04/zansyu/)). Zansyu's crowdfunding licence did not mention web use ([zeczec](https://www.zeczec.com/projects/zansyu)), so it is off the table unless that changes. The 1930s calendar-girl posters are tied to the male gaze ([LSE dissertation](https://www.lse.ac.uk/media-and-communications/assets/documents/research/msc-dissertations/2016/Dissertation-Yifan-Song.pdf)). Two further limits on the evidence: the colour values in the research notes for these artifacts are eyeballed approximations, not measured specifications, and should be sampled from real objects before use. And no award-recognized site was found that animates dumpling folding, steam or seal stamping, so those motion ideas have no precedent.

Motion should follow real cooking verbs, as Kerrygold's does, and stay out of the cook's way. Stamp for tick-off, a pleat that advances with the dumpling steps, and a faint steam plume over the steamer-basket dishes all fit that rule. Heavy food animation has a measured cost: one illustrated food site's animation setup blocked the main thread for **817 ms**, falling to **44 ms** once intro animations ran at first paint and scroll scenes were set up only as the reader approached them ([Renav](https://www.renav.co.uk/case-studies/cozy-cooktoons)). Keep decorative motion off recipe text, out of the focus view, and behind `prefers-reduced-motion`.

The photos fall into two coherent looks: bright daylight with white plates and light wood, or low-key with dark grounds. No single open-licence photographer covers all eight dishes. The closest match is Anna Tarazevich's Pexels series, which spans potstickers, boiled dumplings and pleating hands ([Pexels](https://www.pexels.com/photo/close-up-photo-of-crispy-dumplings-on-a-ceramic-plate-7287716/)). Expect to colour-grade for consistency, which every licence here permits, subject to the BY-SA share-alike condition. Watermarks were screened only at thumbnail size, so check full-resolution corners before publishing.

One honesty issue follows from all this. Google defines the required recipe image as a picture of the completed dish ([Google Search Central](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/recipe)). A stranger's restaurant mapo tofu is a stand-in for Eric's recipe, not a picture of it. That is a judgement call rather than a stated Google rule, but a site that labels recipes *cooked once* or *family-approved* should label stand-in photos too. It should switch the JSON-LD image to Eric's own photo as soon as one exists.

## Conclusion

The numbered ingredient set is the hinge of the whole design. It is the bilingual cookbook's oldest convention, and it also answers the web's best-documented recipe UX failure, losing the amounts mid-method. Build the data model around sets that steps reference by number, and cook mode, scaling, print and structured data all come from one source instead of four. The second insight is that this site's distinctiveness cannot come from borrowed symbols. Open fonts, open photos and award-site palettes are scaffolding available to anyone. Eric's real advantages are his family's own names for the dishes, their handwriting and their corrections ("less sugar"). The Tong Heng rule, keep the real family artifact and modernize around it, makes that the brand. Everyday Chinese information design, the order form, the grid paper and the wall menu, supplies a visual system for it without costume.

Several things remain unknown and are worth checking before committing. No source has tested how current screen readers handle pinyin ruby or vulgar fractions such as ½. No user research compares one-step-at-a-time views with a full list. Wei-Chuan's actual interior layouts were not seen; the Internet Archive scan of *Chinese Snacks* is the place to settle whether its Chinese and English run side by side or stacked, and how its numbered sets look ([Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/chinesesnackswei0000huan)). And every cultural colour value in the research is an approximation to be sampled from real objects.
